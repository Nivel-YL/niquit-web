> Nicotine pouches like ZYN feel like a smarter way to quit smoking. Here's what the actual research says about whether switching really works.

You've tried the patch. You've tried the gum. Maybe you white-knuckled it for three days and caved on the fourth. Now a friend hands you a can of ZYN and says just switch, it's basically the same nicotine without the smoke. So can you quit smoking by switching to nicotine pouches? The honest answer, based on actual clinical trials rather than marketing copy, is more complicated than it sounds.

## Do nicotine pouches actually help you quit smoking?

Not in the way most people assume. A 2025 review by Heshmati and colleagues, published in Addiction, looked at seven randomized trials involving 269 adult participants and found no evidence that tobacco-free nicotine pouches increase actual smoking cessation rates compared with other products or a control group.

That doesn't mean they're useless. The same research found pouches appear well tolerated and may reduce how many cigarettes someone smokes, with effects similar to what's seen with snus or nicotine gum. So they might help you cut down. Quitting entirely is a different, harder claim, and one the current evidence doesn't support yet.

## How do nicotine pouches compare to cigarettes when a craving hits?

This is where a lot of people get tripped up. Heshmati's team found that among current tobacco users, cigarettes were rated more satisfying and reduced craving for nicotine more effectively than pouches did. Intent to reuse the product told the same story: 14 to 46% for nicotine pouches versus 57% for cigarettes.

Part of this comes down to chemistry, not willpower. A pharmacokinetic study found that time to peak nicotine concentration is much longer with pouches, around 32.5 to 34.4 minutes, compared to just 8.5 minutes for a cigarette. Research published in Internal and Emergency Medicine notes that modern pouches can deliver nicotine efficiently enough to ease cravings and displace cigarettes, but at a much slower rate than smoking. If you're used to the fast hit of a cigarette, a pouch is going to feel like it's working in slow motion. That's not a flaw exactly, it's just a different tool, and it explains why a lot of people who try switching feel underwhelmed in the first week.

## Are nicotine pouches safer than cigarettes?

On the toxin front, yes, mostly. Pouches contain nicotine levels similar to cigarettes but no tobacco, and they skip the combustion process that produces most of smoking's hazardous chemicals, according to Heshmati's Addiction review. Limited data from three small studies reviewed by Hartmann-Boyce and colleagues in the Cochrane Library found no short-term serious health harms among smokers who used oral nicotine pouches.

But "no tobacco" doesn't mean "no chemicals of concern." A 2022 analysis in Tobacco Control examined 44 nicotine pouches from 20 manufacturers and found that 26 of them contained known carcinogenic chemicals also found in tobacco products. ## Can you get addicted to nicotine pouches instead of cigarettes?

Yes, and this is the part people underestimate most. Nicotine is addictive regardless of the delivery method. Dr. Ryan Marino, a medical toxicologist quoted by University Hospitals, has pointed out that pouches carry the same risk of dependence, withdrawal, and addiction as any other nicotine product. Recovery Centers of America puts it plainly: pouches eliminate combustion-related toxins, but they still deliver a nicotine dose that can create dependence comparable to cigarettes.

There's also a quieter risk that doesn't get talked about enough. Research from Eternal Purpose Recovery notes that using pouches in addition to smoking, rather than instead of it, doesn't give you any harm reduction at all. It just adds a second source of nicotine throughout your day on top of the cigarettes you're still smoking. ## So should you switch to nicotine pouches to quit smoking?

Here's the honest comparison. A 2022 systematic review of 133 trials on nicotine replacement therapies, published on PMC, found these products can boost successful quit rates by 50 to 60% among smokers who are already motivated to quit. That's a strong, well-established number, but it applies to approved NRT products like patches and gum, not specifically to nicotine pouches, which haven't been through the same volume of testing.

Researchers writing in Internal and Emergency Medicine describe pouches as a promising harm reduction tool with a risk profile likely adjacent to pharmaceutical NRT, something clinicians might consider as a substitute for smokers who are unable or unwilling to quit outright. Current population data also suggests pouches are unlikely to become a gateway to smoking, though the same researchers stress that continuous monitoring is warranted and that doctors should start recording pouch use in patient histories. The Cochrane Library is blunt about where things stand: more research is urgently needed, and future trials need to compare pouches against other active options like NRT or e-cigarettes rather than against nothing.

If you're weighing pouches as a stepping stone off cigarettes, that's a reasonable harm reduction move for some people. Just go in knowing it's a swap, not a cure, and that the goal further down the road is getting off nicotine entirely.

> Nicotine and sleep don't mix well, before or after quitting. Here's why you get insomnia, night sweats, and vivid dreams, and when it ends.

You fall asleep fine. Then you're awake at 3am, sheets damp, heart going a little fast, and you just came out of a dream so strange you're still thinking about it an hour later. This is what nicotine does to sleep, both while you're using it and while you're quitting it, and the science behind it explains almost everything you're feeling right now.

## Why can't I sleep when I'm still smoking or vaping?

Nicotine is a stimulant. That's the short version. It activates your sympathoadrenal system, the part of your body responsible for the fight-or-flight response, which raises your heart rate, blood pressure, and blood sugar right when you're trying to wind down for the night (NHANES analysis via PMC, 2020). Your brain and body are told to stay alert at the exact moment they should be shutting off.

It goes deeper than just feeling wired. Nicotine ramps up activity in the prefrontal cortex, thalamus, and visual system, and that can throw off your circadian rhythm, the internal clock that tells you when to feel sleepy and when to wake up (American Association of Sleep Technologists, 2025). A Lausanne study found this is dose-dependent: the more someone smokes, the faster their brainwaves get during sleep, with less deep delta-wave activity and more alpha-wave activity, the pattern associated with being alert rather than resting (American Association of Sleep Technologists citing Lausanne study, 2025).

The result is a sleep profile that looks like this: it takes longer to fall asleep, you wake up more through the night, you get less deep slow-wave sleep, your overall sleep efficiency drops, and you're groggier the next day (Sleep journal review, 2009). A 2020 Canadian study found smokers are 47% more likely to report sleep-related issues than non-smokers (American Association of Sleep Technologists, 2025). And timing matters: a 2021 study tracking over 1,000 adults aged 22 to 60 found that smoking later at night made insomnia and short sleep duration worse, not just more likely (American Association of Sleep Technologists, 2025).

If you've been noticing you sleep worse the later you have your last cigarette or vape of the day, that's not in your head.

## Why do I keep having such intense dreams since I quit?

Here's something most people don't expect: nicotine actually suppresses REM sleep, the stage where most vivid dreaming happens (Sleep journal review, 2009). So while you're using nicotine, you get less dream sleep overall. But a 2006 study using 24-hour nicotine patches on 15 smokers found something strange: even though the patch group had more awakenings and micro-arousals, the dreams they did have contained more visual imagery and were rated as more vivid (Physiol Behav, 2006). Nicotine doesn't just cut REM sleep short, it seems to distort what happens during the REM you get left.

Once you quit, the dreams often get even weirder. This is called REM rebound. Your brain spent months or years having its REM sleep suppressed, and once nicotine is gone, it overcorrects, packing in longer and more intense REM periods to make up for lost time (Biology Insights, 2025). That's why an estimated 60 to 70% of people who quit report distinct "withdrawal dreams," the kind that feel unusually bizarre or emotionally loaded (Biology Insights, 2025).

This isn't unique to cold-turkey quitters, either. A 2016 study found that over 16% of people using nicotine patches reported vivid dreams, with a similar rate among people combining patches with lozenges (Doctar.in citing 2016 study, 2026). A separate 2017 study of a cessation program using varenicline, patches, and lozenges found that 72% of participants reported abnormal dreams, and seven people actually changed their medication because of it (Healthline citing 2017 study, 2026). If your dreams have gotten stranger since you started quitting, or even since you switched to a patch, you're in very well-documented company.

The good news is this fades. Sleep architecture and dream intensity generally settle back to a healthier baseline within four to eight weeks, though the occasional vivid dream can stick around longer for some people (Biology Insights, 2025).

## Why am I sweating at night after quitting nicotine?

Night sweats are a recognized part of nicotine withdrawal, alongside irritability, cravings, and anxiety (Bedfans-USA blog, 2026; Medical News Today, 2024). Your body has spent a long time adjusting to regular nicotine, and pulling it away suddenly forces your nervous system to recalibrate, and sweating is one of the more physical ways that shows up.

Timing-wise, withdrawal symptoms typically start 4 to 24 hours after your last use, peak around day 3, then ease off gradually over the next three to four weeks (Medical News Today, 2024). Night sweats specifically tend to last anywhere from a few weeks to a few months before disappearing (Drug Test Panels, 2026). ## Does insomnia after quitting ever actually end?

Yes, and it's worth knowing that upfront because insomnia is one of the reasons people go back to smoking or vaping. A meta-analysis of 52 studies found that insomnia during withdrawal can make relapse more likely (Jaehne et al., 2009 via MedRxiv preprint, 2024), and insomnia itself is a clinically recognized withdrawal symptom, not just a side effect people happen to mention (Nicotine & Tobacco Research, 2019).

But sleep quality typically starts improving within 1 to 3 weeks of quitting, even though the withdrawal period itself might make things temporarily worse before that (Nectr Energy). Increased dreaming is actually listed as one of the expected withdrawal symptoms during this window, so the vivid dreams and the improving sleep are often happening at the same time, just on different timelines.

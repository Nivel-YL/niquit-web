You're three days into quitting and it hits: a wave of wanting so strong you'd trade your car for a cigarette. Individual cravings usually pass in 3 to 5 minutes, according to Ireland's Health Service Executive. The problem isn't the craving itself. It's what happens in your head while you're waiting those 5 minutes out.

## How long does a single craving actually last?

A craving is not a mood that settles in for the afternoon. It's a spike. HSE data puts the average individual craving at 3 to 5 minutes from start to fade, and that number matters more than almost anything else in early quitting. If you can find something to do for 5 minutes, walk to another room, drink water, text someone, the craving that felt unbearable at second 10 is often gone by second 240.

The reason it feels endless is different from the reason it exists. Nicotine binds to receptors in your brain that trigger the release of dopamine and serotonin, chemicals tied to reward and calm, according to Drugs.com (2025). When the nicotine stops coming, those receptors sit empty, and your brain reads the gap as an emergency. That urgency is real. It's just short-lived, biologically speaking, even when it doesn't feel that way in the moment. If you want the full mechanics of why the urgency feels so disproportionate to the actual danger, we've broken it down in [why nicotine cravings feel so urgent and how to wait them out](/blog/why-nicotine-cravings-feel-so-urgent-and-how-to-wait-them-out).

## When do cravings start after you quit, and when do they peak?

Cravings don't wait long to show up. Cravings for nicotine can start 30 minutes after your last cigarette, according to Ireland's Health Service Executive. The symptoms of nicotine withdrawal usually appear 2–3 hours after the last intake of nicotine, according to Wikipedia's medically-cited overview (2024).

The peak is the part most people underestimate. Cleveland Clinic (2026) places the worst of it on day two or three of being nicotine-free, with symptoms fading gradually over the following three to four weeks. Nicotine withdrawal symptoms are typically at their worst during the first week of quitting, especially the first 3 to 5 days, according to Drugs.com. That's an important distinction. The sharp, physical craving and the quieter emotional habit of reaching for nicotine are not the same thing, and they don't disappear on the same timeline. ## Why do cravings keep coming back even after the first week?

This is where people get discouraged and think something's wrong with them. It isn't. A 2001 review in a peer-reviewed pharmacology journal (PubMed) found that everyday things, places, people, routines, can become linked to nicotine use through conditioning, and that this link is mediated by a brain region called the ventral tegmental area. In plain terms: your brain doesn't just crave nicotine chemically, it learns to associate specific triggers (your morning coffee, a stressful call, a certain friend) with using it. Those learned triggers can fire off cravings long after the chemical withdrawal has settled.

Functional neuroimaging studies have identified a core network of craving-related brain responses to smoking cues that comprises the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and ventral striatum. This is why a craving can appear out of nowhere weeks into quitting, triggered by something as small as a smell or a familiar street corner. ## When do cravings finally stop for good?

The honest answer: gradually, and unevenly. Ireland's Health Service Executive states that cravings generally improve 4 to 6 weeks after quitting. Nicotine cravings can last from a few hours to several weeks, with significant reduction typically occurring within the first 7 to 10 weeks after cessation, according to Consensus Academic Search.

More than 70% of people who quit smoking experience cravings and increased appetite during this window, according to Tobacco Free Life (2016), so if you're white-knuckling through week two, you're not the exception, you're the norm.

For a minority of people, cravings don't fully disappear, they can persist in a milder form for years, according to the National Cancer Institute. But there's real relief in that fact too. According to the journal Addiction, nicotine withdrawal is less likely than some other drug withdrawal syndromes to produce protracted, neonatal, or precipitated withdrawal symptoms. The occasional craving five years out is not the same beast as the withdrawal in week one. It's an echo, not a wave.

## What actually helps in the 3 to 5 minute window?

Knowing a craving is temporary doesn't make it comfortable, but it changes what you do with it. Instead of asking "how do I make this stop," the better question is "what do I do for the next 5 minutes." Change your environment, move your body, drink something cold, call someone. The goal isn't to defeat the craving. It's to outlast it, because biologically, it's already on a clock.

If cravings are showing up around specific triggers rather than randomly, it's worth mapping those triggers directly rather than treating every craving as identical. That distinction, chemical withdrawal versus conditioned habit, is often the difference between a quit attempt that stalls at week two and one that makes it past week ten.

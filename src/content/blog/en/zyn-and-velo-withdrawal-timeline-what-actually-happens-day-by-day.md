> ZYN and Velo withdrawal timeline explained hour by hour: what happens on day 1, why day 3 is brutal, and when cravings finally fade.

You popped your last ZYN or Velo pouch a few hours ago, and already your jaw feels weird, your mind is restless, and you're wondering if this was a mistake. It wasn't. This is just the beginning of the ZYN and Velo withdrawal timeline, and knowing what's coming next makes it a lot easier to sit through.

Nicotine pouches work fast. A pouch sits between your gum and cheek, and nicotine gets absorbed straight through the tissue there, no smoke, no vapor involved (Birches Health, 2026). That direct route, combined with pH adjusters in the pouch that speed absorption into your bloodstream, is why a 6 mg ZYN can put more nicotine into your system after 30 minutes than an actual cigarette (Forbes, 2026). Your body got used to that. Now it's asking for it back.

## How long does ZYN or Velo withdrawal actually last?

Nicotine has a half-life of roughly two hours, which means it clears out of your blood fast, according to a pharmacology review by Neal Benowitz and colleagues. By the end of day one without a pouch, most of it is already gone. But withdrawal symptoms don't need nicotine to hit zero to start. According to Cleveland Clinic, symptoms typically begin somewhere between 4 and 24 hours after your last dose if you've been using regularly (Cleveland Clinic, 2026).

From there, the general shape looks like this: symptoms build fast, peak hard around day two or three, then slowly loosen their grip over the following weeks. Cleveland Clinic notes that most people see symptoms fade out over 3 to 4 weeks (Cleveland Clinic, 2026). That's the whole arc. What happens inside that window is where things get specific.

## What happens on day 1 after you quit ZYN or Velo?

Day one is deceptively manageable for some people and rough for others, depending on how much you were using and how your body handles the drop. Blood nicotine is already crashing, but your brain hasn't fully caught up yet. You might notice a low hum of irritability, maybe a headache starting to form, maybe just a nagging sense that something's missing from your routine.

This is also when the psychological habit starts pushing back. If you were using pouches constantly throughout the day, the ritual of reaching for one, tucking it in, feeling it settle, that muscle memory doesn't disappear just because the nicotine is leaving your bloodstream.

## Why is day 3 the hardest?

By day two or three, nicotine is long gone from your blood, and this is exactly when things get worst. Cleveland Clinic points to day two or three as the peak for withdrawal intensity (Cleveland Clinic, 2026): the nicotine has cleared, but your brain hasn't adjusted to its absence yet.

Expect a cluster of unpleasant but temporary symptoms. Cravings, irritability, headaches, trouble concentrating and trouble sleeping are all on the Cleveland Clinic's list of common withdrawal symptoms. The cravings tend to come in waves, and each one passes in three to five minutes whether you use or not, according to the American Lung Association.

Underneath all of this is a chemical shift. Nicotine artificially raises dopamine in the brain's reward pathways, which is what made it feel good in the first place (New York State Department of Health). Take that away, and dopamine activity in the nucleus accumbens drops below your normal baseline, which raises your reward threshold, meaning ordinary things feel less satisfying for a while, and can produce depression-like symptoms until your brain recalibrates (Journal of Neuroscience, 2025). It's not in your head, it's chemistry catching up.

A review in Nicotine & Tobacco Research backs this up: irritability, anxiety, low mood, trouble concentrating, restlessness and poor sleep are the core withdrawal symptoms, and they peak in the first week. If you're struggling more with anxiety specifically, the deeper mechanics are covered in [quitting nicotine and anxiety: what is normal and what to do](/blog/quitting-nicotine-and-anxiety-what-is-normal-and-what-to-do).

## What happens in week 2 to week 4?

Once you're past day 4, things start to loosen a bit. After the third day, symptoms get a little better every day, according to the Cleveland Clinic, though poor sleep and a bigger appetite are still common during this stretch. You might sleep worse than usual for a few nights or find yourself hungrier than normal. Both tend to settle.

Weeks 2 through 4 mark a real turning point. The physical symptoms, the headaches, the restlessness, the acute irritability, start easing off, but psychological cravings can stick around longer. This is the phase where people often think they're "over it" and then get blindsided by a craving triggered by stress, boredom, or a specific situation where they used to reach for a pouch. That's normal. Your brain is still winding down the extra nicotine receptors it built up. Brain-imaging studies of people who quit smoking have found receptor levels back to non-smoker levels anywhere from about three weeks to three months after quitting. Those studies looked at cigarettes, but nicotine acts on the same receptors whatever the product.

## Why pouches are trickier to quit than they look

Part of what makes this timeline harder to navigate with pouches specifically is how invisible the addiction was in the first place. Detox to Rehab points out that pouch addiction often develops more quietly than cigarette or vape addiction, since there's no smoke, no ash, no vapor cloud to signal how often you're actually using (Detox to Rehab, 2026). That can mean near-constant use throughout the day, which builds a stronger dependence than it feels like it should.

There's also no FDA-approved product designed to help you quit pouches. The FDA has authorized a number of nicotine pouches for sale, but not one of them as a quitting tool (Truth Initiative, 2026). You're managing this withdrawal on willpower and strategy, not a sanctioned taper product.

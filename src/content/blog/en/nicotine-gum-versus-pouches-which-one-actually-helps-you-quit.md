> Nicotine gum has 56 clinical trials behind it. Pouches have almost none. Here's what the evidence actually says about which one helps you quit.

You're standing in the pharmacy aisle, or scrolling an online store, trying to decide between a pack of nicotine gum and a tin of pouches. Both promise to help you quit. Only one of them has decades of research proving it does.

Nicotine gum versus pouches isn't really a fair fight when you look at the evidence. Gum has been studied in 56 trials involving more than 22,000 people. Pouches, by comparison, are the new kid who showed up with confidence but no transcript.

## Do nicotine pouches actually help you quit smoking?

Here's the uncomfortable answer: nobody really knows yet.

A 2025 systematic review of randomized trials on tobacco-free nicotine pouches found that while they appear well tolerated and may reduce how many cigarettes people smoke, there's currently no statistically significant evidence that they increase actual quit rates compared to gum, snus, or even placebo (PubMed/Cochrane systematic review, 2025). A separate Cochrane review from the same year went further, stating that the evidence is simply uncertain whether oral nicotine pouches help people quit smoking at all compared to just telling someone to keep smoking as usual (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Hartmann-Boyce et al., 2025).

Research out of Addiction journal, done with the Ottawa Heart Institute, backs this up: pouches may help some people cut down on cigarettes, but there's no evidence they outperform the nicotine replacement therapies that have been proven in large trials and used in clinical care for decades (Addiction, Ottawa Heart Institute, 2024). The same research group found the reduction in cigarettes per day from pouches is similar to what you'd get from other nicotine products, not better.

If you're curious how pouches compare to cigarettes in raw nicotine terms, that's covered in more detail in [how much nicotine is actually in a pouch compared to a cigarette](/blog/how-much-nicotine-is-actually-in-a-pouch-compared-to-a-cigarette).

## Is nicotine gum better than pouches for quitting?

On the data we have, yes. By a wide margin.

A landmark Cochrane review by Hartmann-Boyce and colleagues looked at 56 trials and over 22,500 participants and found nicotine gum increases the odds of quitting with a risk ratio of 1.49, meaning people using gum were about 49% more likely to successfully quit than those who weren't (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Hartmann-Boyce et al., 2018). Across nicotine replacement therapies broadly, the same body of Cochrane research found a 50% to 60% increase in the chances of stopping smoking (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2018).

Gum also has something pouches don't: FDA approval specifically as a smoking cessation aid. Tobacco-free nicotine pouches are not approved by the FDA for this purpose (multiple FDA regulatory sources). That distinction matters more than it sounds. Approval means a product went through trials designed specifically to test whether it helps people stop, not just whether people like using it.

There's also an older but still relevant finding worth mentioning: among the most heavily dependent smokers, specifically those who crave a cigarette the moment they wake up, 4mg gum was found to be the most effective form of replacement therapy, helping around one in three of them quit (BMJ/PubMed, 1996).

## Why do pouches feel stronger than gum if they don't work as well?

This is where it gets interesting, and where a lot of confusion comes from.

A randomized crossover trial comparing a 4mg nicotine pouch to 4mg nicotine gum found the pouch produced a peak blood nicotine level roughly twice as high as the gum, about 8.5 ng/mL versus 4.4 ng/mL, and delivered roughly double the total nicotine exposure over the same period (Scientific Reports, 2022). That's a real, measurable difference in how much nicotine hits your bloodstream.

So pouches deliver nicotine faster and in a bigger dose. That might feel more satisfying in the moment, closer to the hit you got from a cigarette. But satisfying a craving and actually quitting are two different outcomes. A product that delivers more nicotine more efficiently doesn't automatically help you need nicotine less. If anything, it can reinforce the same dependence loop you're trying to break, just through a different delivery method. That's part of why switching from cigarettes to pouches often becomes its own addiction rather than an exit from one. If that sounds familiar, [can you quit smoking by switching to nicotine pouches](/blog/can-you-quit-smoking-by-switching-to-nicotine-pouches) digs into exactly this trap.

Gum, by contrast, was designed and tested as a cessation tool from the start. The dosing is more controlled, and the chewing mechanism itself, slower absorption through the lining of your mouth, roughly matches the pace your cravings actually need to be managed, not spiked.

## So which one should you actually use?

If your goal is specifically to quit, not to switch what you're addicted to, gum has the track record. Fifty-six trials and a documented 49% increase in quit success rate isn't nothing. Pouches might reduce how much you smoke in the short term, which has some value, but reducing isn't the same as quitting, and the current evidence doesn't show they get you across that finish line more often than gum, snus, or even no help at all.

If you do decide to use pouches as a stepping stone, treat them the way you'd treat any nicotine product: as something you need a plan to get off of eventually, not a permanent replacement. [Tapering off nicotine pouches](/blog/tapering-off-nicotine-pouches-does-cutting-down-actually-work) walks through whether cutting down actually works in practice, because for a lot of people it quietly doesn't.

Either way, the honest answer to "gum or pouches" right now is: one of them has been tested to actually help you stop, and the other is still waiting for its own proof.

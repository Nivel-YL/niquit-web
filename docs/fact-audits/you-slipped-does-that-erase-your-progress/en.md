# Fact Audit: you-slipped-does-that-erase-your-progress (EN)
Generated: 2026-09-14

===SOURCE_TABLE===
F1|Oar Health|2|ok|According to Oar Health, a slip (or lapse) is when you've been smoke-free for a while and then smoke once or twice.
F2|Oar Health|2|ok|Oar Health defines a slip specifically as the first time you smoke at all after quitting, which sounds alarming until you realize that "first time" doesn't have to mean "only time you tried" or "back to square one."
F2|Oar Health|2|ok|Having one cigarette or a couple of puffs after quitting doesn't have to ruin your quit attempt.
F4|PubMed (Marlatt & Gordon)|1|ok|Research analyzed by Marlatt and Gordon (PubMed, 1990) followed 216 slip-up episodes and found that 44 of them, roughly 20%, resulted in a return to abstinence rather than a full relapse.
F7|PMC|1|ok|A 2023 study on a smoking cessation smartphone app (PMC) found that most people who experience an initial lapse do progress to full relapse, and that transition takes about 19 days on average.
F5|PubMed|1|ok|The same 1990 research found that slips happening in a good mood, while socializing and drinking, and after more than two weeks of abstinence were actually associated with higher rates of getting back to abstinence, not lower.
F6|PMC|1|ok|A 2024 meta-analysis using ecological momentary assessment (PMC) found that environmental cues, social situations, and cravings are the main triggers behind lapses in the moment.
F10|PMC|1|ok|A 2019 analysis published on PMC found that the most common causes of relapse are weight gain, stress, and nicotine withdrawal symptoms, not a single moment of weakness.
F11|ScienceDirect|1|ok|A 2023 study on Chinese adult smokers (ScienceDirect) tracked relapse probability over time and found it drops sharply the longer you go: 49.07% at 2 years, down to 20.05% at 4 years, 10.29% at 6 years, and 6.63% at 8 years.
F12|Royal College of Nursing and Midwifery Journal|1|ok|Separately, research published in the Royal College of Nursing and Midwifery Journal (2023) found that relapse risk drops notably once you pass 6 months smoke-free, which is why the 6-month mark is often used as a real benchmark for long-term success.
F13|EX Community|2|ok|According to the EX Community (2023), after a slip you haven't unlearned everything you've learned, and you haven't unchanged everything you've changed in your life to support quitting.
F14|Mayo Clinic|1|ok|The Mayo Clinic notes that virtually all successful quitters had unsuccessful attempts before the one that worked.
F8|NIH (TRIUMPH)|1|ok|A NIH clinical trial protocol from 2013 (TRIUMPH) puts a number on it: the average smoker makes 5 attempts before quitting for good.
F15|NIH|1|ok|And separate NIH research on postpartum smoking cessation states plainly that having one lapse, or even a few, is a normal part of the quitting process, not evidence that the process failed.
F9|PMC|1|ok|PMC research from 2019 found that three months of professional cessation treatment gets about 80% of people smoke-free initially, but by the one-year mark that number settles closer to 50%.
